Bhandari (2016) defines a convention center as any property utilized for an event concerning a conference, meeting center, or any similar facility; this includes administrative offices, exposition halls, auditoriums, and facilities for preparing and serving food. Sung & Lee (2015), on the other hand, defines a convention center as a sizable building that is designed to hold conventions, exhibitions, trade shows, as well as other events.

Because the conference industry is comparatively young, its terminology remains loosely defined; the term 'convention center' is sometimes used interchangeably with 'exhibition center' and related terms such as congress, assembly, or symposium, yet these venues share a common purpose: bringing people together for the exchange of ideas and socialization (Rogers & Wynn-Moylan, 2022; Sharma, 2025).

Convention centers play a significant part in the MICE industry (Meetings, Incentives, Conferences, and Exhibitions), where event organizers, suppliers, and attendees gather for academic, professional, cultural, or business purposes (Wee, Kamarulzaman, & Anas, 2022). Because a single venue must host this breadth of gatherings, its interior must accommodate a wide range of spatial and functional demands, placing the convention center's internal configuration, rather than its economic output, at the center of its usefulness.

## MICE Industry

The sustained global growth of the MICE industry has driven continued investment in convention infrastructure (Allied Market Research, 2019), and with that growth have come rising expectations for the venues themselves. What increasingly distinguishes a competitive venue is less its economic scale than the quality and adaptability of its interior environment.

Multiple studies have noted that governments all across the globe have recognized the potential of the MICE industry, which has then led to investments in buildings for MICE venues (Lee & Fenich, 2016). However, as the MICE industry evolves through time, older facilities are now subjected to the pressure to modernize and adapt to meet the changing expectations of both the event organizers and attendees. Studies emphasized how important aligning the facilities with client demands is; this would include flexibility, cleanliness, technological integration, sustainability, and accessibility (An, Kim, & Hur, 2021).

The MICE industry can be correlated with event spaces in general, as it is essentially a space for various events. With this, a major consideration for the success of this industry would be the decision-making process of event organizers or planners, who act on behalf of their clients. These professionals consider various factors when it comes to selecting a venue, some of which would include the location, accessibility, and facilities (Lee & Lee, 2017). Additionally, adaptable room sizes, advanced audio-visual equipment, and user-friendly layouts play critical roles in ensuring long-term competitiveness in the MICE industry (Anas et al., 2020).

## Modularity, Flexibility, and Adaptability

Modular design is a design strategy and common trend in convention center designs that involves either the combination or the separation of areas to cater to different kinds of events (Barr, 2023). Modular design also plays an important role when it comes to enhancing a convention center’s flexibility and adaptability.

Flexibility and adaptability fall under the umbrella of modular design, wherein modular systems inherently support spatial reconfiguration, event customization, and functional upgrades without requiring substantial structural changes. Hashemian (2005) explains that modularity allows changes in one area to remain localized, thereby making a system easier to adapt. She further states that:

> “A product with a segmented architecture is generally easier to modify, hence has greater adaptability.”

This segmentation can then enable a space to respond to varying functional demands actively, thereby reducing the need for rigid and fixed layouts.

Convention Centers are essentially multi-use spaces that cater to a diverse array of events, from corporate conferences and trade shows to cultural exhibits and community assemblies (Ike et al., 2023; Van der Wagen & White, 2017). To remain competitive and relevant in the evolving MICE industry, the venues should be designed with the future in mind. Hence, modular design becomes a strategic approach that facilitates the seamless transformation of spaces through flexible furniture clusters, adjustable seating, convertible stages, and technological integration (Ike et al., 2023; FXCollaborative, 2022).

In this context:

* **Modularity** provides the physical framework through which change is made possible.
* **Flexibility** enables the day-to-day reconfiguration of space to suit different activities.
* **Adaptability** ensures that the design can evolve, accommodating new technologies, user expectations, and environmental conditions.

With this in mind, modularity, flexibility, and adaptability are connected. Together, they allow convention centers to become resilient, sustainable, and user-centric environments that support a wide range of functions without compromising performance or identity (Hashemian, 2005; Ike et al., 2023). As emphasized in multiple studies, prioritizing flexibility through modular and adaptive design strategies will not only boost functionality but also promote ecological and economic growth, making these venues truly future-proof.

## Future-Proof Design

Future-Proof design refers to the strategic planning and creation of built environments that remain relevant, functional, and adaptable in the face of evolving needs and conditions. In architectural and interior design practice, designing with future-proofing in mind anticipates changes in technology, demographics, and environmental conditions (DesignHorizons, 2024). This approach ensures that spaces do not become obsolete but instead continue to serve users effectively over time.

A core principle of future-proofing is versatility and flexibility, which allows spaces to be reconfigured or repurposed without requiring significant structural changes. With the rapid development of society and technology, public spaces must be prepared to accommodate different functional requirements and usage methods at different times (Dong, Ibrahim, & Azahari, 2024). This might involve modular layouts, reconfigurable furniture, smart infrastructure, or adaptable lighting and acoustic systems.

By considering long-term trends, such as climate change, population growth, digital transformation, and shifts in work and lifestyle habits, future-proof design fosters sustainability and resilience. It minimizes the need for costly renovations, reduces environmental impact, and supports inclusivity and accessibility for a diverse range of users.

Ultimately, future-proofing embeds adaptability into the design itself, allowing spaces to remain functional and relevant as user needs, technologies, and environmental conditions change over time.

## Adaptable & Flexible Design

Multiple studies have repeatedly highlighted two key design elements essential when designing adaptable and flexible spaces: technological integration, particularly in lighting and environmental control, and the use of movable furniture and partitions to transform spaces. Features like adjustable and dimmable lighting fixtures and smart lighting systems allow for the ambiance of a space to be tailored to suit different events. From an environmental design perspective, these technologies not only enhance visual comfort but also contribute to energy efficiency and user satisfaction by intelligently responding to the type of event or occupancy levels (Dong, Ibrahim, & Azahari, 2024; as supported by industry practice insights in DesignHorizons, 2024). Moreover, Pembegül (2009) states that:

> “Interior design and technological equipment should meet the contemporary requirements of convention activities.”

Additionally, acoustics and spatial adaptability are just as essential. In applied venue fit-outs, the incorporation of movable acoustic panels made from sustainable materials like recycled felt or cork supports effective sound management in multipurpose spaces, ensuring the functionality of the space across various events (DesignHorizons, 2024). Moreover, adjustable furniture and fixtures, such as foldable tables, stackable chairs, and reconfigurable seating modules, provide spatial flexibility by allowing users to reconfigure the space quickly and efficiently. These elements can support transitions between open and private setups, catering to a wide range of user needs, from professional meetings to social gatherings (Dong, Ibrahim, & Azahari, 2024).

Acoustic performance is equally central to a reconfigurable event hall, and recent studies treat it as a spatial and architectural design problem rather than a purely engineering one. Cairoli (2020) documents a convention-center auditorium reconfigured with movable curtains, pivoting wood panels, and adjustable ceiling plates to shift between speech-oriented congress and music-oriented concert settings, while Habibi (2025) shows how curved panels and an adjustable ceiling can alter room volume to suit dual speech-and-music functions. Passive variable systems, operable panels and retractable elements that change a room's absorption, have been shown to meaningfully affect reverberation and speech intelligibility in multipurpose halls. At the material scale, hybrid absorptive–diffusive wooden panels with interchangeable overlays allow a surface's absorption class to be adjusted using sustainable, FSC-certified timber (Chojnacki et al., 2023, 2024), aligning acoustic control with the study's future-proof and sustainable-material goals.

## Anthropometric and Ergonomic Standards

To develop a highly adaptable, culturally responsive, and operationally efficient interior design framework for the Calbayog City Convention Center’s 840 sqm interior envelope (711 sqm Main Hall and 129 sqm Foyer), the spatial layouts must be grounded in human scale, physiological capabilities, and regulatory compliance. These standards dictate the limits of physical comfort, universal accessibility, and egress safety within the interior envelope.

In public assembly interiors, human body dimensions govern the design of furniture, fixtures, and equipment (FF&E), along with the spatial clearances surrounding them. Anthropometry is broadly categorized into two fields of study, both of which are critical to the execution of flexible interior layouts:

* **Static (Structural) Anthropometry**: The measurement of the human body at rest in standardized, stationary positions. These metrics dictate the dimensions of individual seating modules, table heights, and structural sightlines.
* **Dynamic (Functional) Anthropometry**: The measurement of the body in motion, describing the spatial envelopes required for physical activities, reach zones, and movement patterns. These metrics dictate the clearance corridors between seating rows, the walking paths of service staff, and the physical force required to rearrange modular interior systems.

The 711 sqm Main Hall and 129 sqm Foyer of the Calbayog City Convention Center must morph seamlessly between diverse layout configurations and pre-function staging. To make these reconfigurations viable, the physical layout templates must integrate the anthropometric clearances established by Panero and Zelnik (1979) and Neufert (2012).

### A. Theater (Plenary) Layout Typology

* **Seat Pitch (Row-to-Row Distance)**: The minimum distance from backrest to backrest must be calibrated to ensure both seated thigh clearance and emergency passage. Under standard conditions (Neufert, 2012), a seat pitch of 900 mm (35.4 in) is required. This provides a clear path of at least 305 mm (12 in) between the front edge of one seat cushion (when down) and the back of the seat in front.
* **Lateral Seat Allocation**: To comfortably accommodate 95th percentile male shoulder breadth and hip width, individual seat modules must have a minimum width of 500 mm (19.7 in), with 550 mm (21.6 in) preferred for executive event formats.
* **Sightline Clearance (C-Value)**: The vertical sightline parameter must allow an observer to view the performance over the head of the person sitting directly in front. The standard vertical offset (C-value) between rows of seating is calculated at 120 mm (4.7 in), or 60 mm (2.4 in) with staggered seating layouts.

### B. Classroom (Lecture) Layout Typology

* **Desk-to-Chair Workspace**: To accommodate seated reach and forward-leaning actions, the desk writing surface must be positioned 740 mm to 760 mm (29 in to 30 in) above the floor (Neufert, 2012).
* **User Seat Clearance**: The clearance depth between the front edge of the writing desk and the backrest of the seat behind it must be 450 mm to 500 mm (17.7 in to 19.7 in) to accommodate the abdominal depth and upper torso movement of a 95th-percentile occupant.
* **Service Aisle Clearance**: The corridor behind the occupied chair to the edge of the next desk must be at least 400 mm to 450 mm (15.7 in to 17.7 in). This dynamic clearance allows a service planner or late attendee to traverse the aisle sideways without disrupting seated occupants.

### C. Banquet (Social/Dining) Layout Typology

* **Table Clearance Circles**: The centerpiece of the banquet typology is the standard circular table. For a 10-person round table (1500 mm to 1800 mm / 60 in to 72 in), a total circular spatial footprint of 3200 mm to 3500 mm (126 in to 138 in) must be maintained.
* **Table-to-Table Service Lanes**: The linear passage distance between adjacent circular tables must be a minimum of 1620 mm to 1800 mm (63.8 in to 70.8 in). This spacing accounts for back-to-back seated occupants (each requiring 450 mm of depth from the table edge) plus a central 720 mm to 900 mm walking zone. This central zone accommodates the dynamic body width of servers carrying trays or planners navigating the room.

### D. Exhibition Layout Typology

* **Pedestrian Grid Corridors**: To allow natural, non-congested foot traffic within the 840 sqm interior envelope (711 sqm Main Hall and 129 sqm Foyer) during commercial setups, the circulation aisles between booth modules must be designed in multiples of the human shoulder-width profile.
* **Pedestrian Clearance Multiples**:
  * *Single file passage*: Minimum clear path of 750 mm to 900 mm (29.5 in to 35.4 in) based on maximum body breadth.
  * *Two-person bi-directional passing*: Minimum path of 1500 mm to 1800 mm (59.0 in to 70.8 in).
  * *Three-person crowd capacity corridor*: Minimum path of 2400 mm (94.5 in). This ensures that visitors can stop to engage with an exhibition booth without creating bottlenecks for passing guests.

### Universal Design & Accessibility Compliance

To ensure the Calbayog City Convention Center is inclusive, the interior design overlay must prioritize universal design principles, making the space fully usable for individuals with physical, sensory, or cognitive challenges. Spatial modules must be designed to comply with local accessibility laws, specifically Batas Pambansa Bilang 344 (BP 344), and international benchmarks like the ADA Standards for Accessible Design (2010):

* **Wheelchair Footprint and Clear Path**: Under BP 344, a standard manual wheelchair has a clear structural footprint of 700 mm x 1200 mm (27.5 in x 47.2 in). To allow unhindered movement, walkways, primary ramps, and internal corridors must maintain a minimum clear width of 1200 mm (47.2 in).
* **Wheelchair Turning Space**: To execute a 360° direction change, a wheelchair user requires a turning circle with a minimum diameter of 1500 mm (59.0 in). This space must be built into dead ends, cross-aisles, and modular furniture clusters.
* **Accessible Assembly Seating Dispersal**: BP 344 and the ADA require that dedicated wheelchair parking stalls be seamlessly integrated into the general seating arrangement. These stalls must:
  * Have a minimum clear footprint of 760 mm x 1220 mm (30 in x 48 in) for rear or front-access entry.
  * Be located alongside a general circulation aisle to facilitate easy emergency egress.
  * Provide companion seating immediately adjacent to the wheelchair space.

## Ceiling Design and Rigging Systems in Exhibition and Event Spaces

The ceiling design of interior spaces in large-scale venues like convention centers offers both aesthetic and functional opportunities. In exhibition and commercial event practice, ceiling elements serve not only to conceal unsightly infrastructure like pipes and ductwork but also to elevate the visual and spatial experience (Syma, 2023). Modular ceiling configurations, such as raised ceilings, suspended panels, or ceiling sails, can enhance the branding, spatial legibility, and atmospheric quality of a venue while simultaneously allowing for fire safety and other building regulations to be met. These elements can be customized with mesh materials or branded banners, turning overhead space into an active zone for user engagement and visual communication.

Motorized truss systems add another layer of spatial adaptability. Institutional project reports, such as installations at Hills Adventist College and UNSW's Webster 334, document how overhead rigging can be raised or lowered to reconfigure space and simplify routine maintenance (Cat, 2023; Phantos, 2025). From an interior design standpoint, the primary value of such systems is spatial, visual, and operational rather than purely mechanical; they keep logistical wiring and structural clutter concealed within the upper ceiling envelope, allowing lighting instruments, acoustic drapes, and spatial banners to be repositioned rapidly without disrupting the floor below (Phantos, 2025).

More broadly, technical staging guidelines indicate that overhead rigging and modular truss systems support flexible spatial design by carrying audiovisual hardware—luminaires, acoustic speakers, and projection screens—above head height, thereby freeing the floor plane for more fluid, open circulation across diverse event configurations (AV Alliance, 2024). Because the present study treats the overhead framework as a non-structural interior design and aesthetic interface rather than an engineered mechanical assembly, the relevant lesson from these trade precedents is how overhead flexibility enables rapid atmospheric and logistical reconfiguration, minimizing turnaround bottlenecks between sequential event bookings.

The implementation of these systems supports the vision of future-proofed, modular interior design. By enabling quick reconfiguration, safer installations, and greater spatial responsiveness, these ceiling and rigging solutions play a vital role in the adaptability and sustainability of modern convention centers.

## Operational Turnaround, Spatial Logistics, and Layout Conversion in Multi-Use Venues

While seating typologies and static anthropometric clearances establish the baseline dimensional requirements of event spaces, the operational viability of a multipurpose hall depends on its **turnaround performance**—the speed, logistical labor, and spatial ease with which an interior envelope can be transformed between consecutive event bookings (Lawson, 2000; Lee & Lee, 2017). In multipurpose municipal facilities, room turnaround time serves as a primary indicator of interior layout efficiency and revenue generation capability.

### A. The Turnaround Bottleneck in Municipal Civic Halls
Fred Lawson (2000), the foundational international authority on convention and exhibition facility architecture, identifies room changeover duration as the single most critical operational constraint in multi-use halls. Lawson argues that turnaround delays are primarily spatial and architectural: they stem from inadequate service corridors (recommending unobstructed 1200 mm to 1800 mm transport lanes), poor egress planning, lack of dedicated furniture staging buffers, and manual ad-hoc layout alignment. When spatial dimensions and furniture configurations are unstandardized, event setup crews resort to trial-and-error measurement, resulting in extended labor hours, aisle congestion, and physical fatigue.

Empirical research in event venue planning confirms these operational challenges. Ayuba and Agah (2018), in their assessment of event facilities, observe that although venues require multi-functional flexibility, over 80% lack specialized flexible interior features—such as standardized movable partitions, modular furniture arrangements, and designated staging zones—leading to severe operational friction during spatial conversions. Furthermore, Lee and Lee (2017) along with An, Kim, and Hur (2021) and Pembegül (2009) establish that event planners and organizers prioritize rapid configuration speed, loading ease, and modular furniture adaptability as decisive factors in facility selection.

### B. Facilities Layout, Material Handling, and Spatial Friction
From a facilities planning perspective, Tompkins, White, Bozer, and Tanchoco (2010) and Muther (1973) demonstrate that unstandardized room arrangements generate excessive *transit tortuosity*—disorganized, zig-zag travel paths that multiply the physical material handling workload ($W_{\text{motion}}$) of logistical personnel. Tompkins et al. (2010) establish that establishing designated perimeter staging zones, direct linear transit paths, and standardized furniture clusters directly reduces material handling time and eliminates spatial collisions during high-pressure room turnovers.

### C. Time-and-Motion Observation and Comparative Simulation in Environmental Design
In architectural and interior design research, measuring spatial efficiency through direct observation and simulation is an established empirical method. Zeisel (2006) codifies *physical trace mapping and direct spatial observation* as fundamental environmental design inquiry tools, demonstrating that observing human movement patterns and object manipulation reveals how physical layouts either facilitate or impede human tasks. 

Building on this, Groat and Wang (2013) establish the *simulation and modeling research strategy* in architecture and interior design. They validate that researchers can document baseline empirical parameters (such as elapsed setup durations and labor expenditure under unstandardized conditions) and construct comparative simulation models to test and substantiate that proposed interior layout standards achieve measurable spatial optimizations prior to physical implementation. Combining work study standards (International Labour Office [ILO] / Kanawaty, 1992; Barnes, 1980) with architectural space planning allows interior designers to scientifically quantify turnaround reduction ($\Delta T\%$) and labor-hour savings ($\Delta MH\%$), bridging physical interior design with operational venue performance.

## Related Empirical Studies on Venue Flexibility and Operational Performance

To bridge theoretical spatial principles with operational venue realities, recent empirical studies in convention architecture and facilities planning have investigated the physical parameters governing layout flexibility, organizer decision-making, and turnaround efficiency:

### A. Architectural and Spatial Strategies for Venue Flexibility (Ike et al., 2023)
In an empirical investigation of contemporary event infrastructure, Ike, Barnaby, Agu, and Igwegbe (2023) examined architectural and spatial strategies utilized to optimize multi-functional flexibility in international conference and exhibition centers. Their research demonstrates that venue adaptability is achieved through three primary spatial mechanisms:
1. **Kinetic Spatial Segmentation**: Deploying demountable, high-performance acoustic movable partition systems to rapidly subdivide large assembly halls into independent acoustic zones for simultaneous breakout sessions or parallel exhibitions without permanent wall construction.
2. **Integrated Overhead Infrastructure**: Utilizing ceiling-suspended rigging and utility grids to carry audiovisual hardware, dynamic luminaires, and power distribution, thereby clearing the floor plane of cable hazards and maximizing usable square footage for flexible seating typologies.
3. **Dynamic Circulation Channels**: Standardizing pedestrian circulation corridors to prevent bottlenecks during high-density transitions between plenary assemblies and exhibition booths.

While Ike et al. (2023) analyzed macro-scale international facilities, their findings directly inform the spatial framework of this study. The present research translates these architectural principles into a non-structural interior overlay—combining modular FF&E clusters and an adaptable overhead track system calibrated specifically for an 840 sqm municipal envelope (711 sqm Main Hall and 129 sqm Foyer).

### B. Empirical Assessment of Flexible Features in Event Centres (Ayuba & Agah, 2018)
In an empirical evaluation of multipurpose event venues, Ayuba and Agah (2018) assessed the physical features utilized to achieve spatial flexibility across civic and commercial event halls. Their field findings revealed a critical operational deficiency: although all surveyed facilities operated as multi-use spaces in concept, **over 80% lacked specialized flexible interior features**—such as standardized movable partitions, modular furniture arrangements, and pre-calculated staging zones. Consequently, operators experienced severe operational friction, excessive changeover labor, and damaged finishes during ad-hoc layout conversions. Ayuba and Agah concluded that achieving genuine multi-functionality requires purpose-designed modular furniture systems and clear spatial layout guides rather than open, unconfigured floor plans alone.

### C. Organizer Priorities and Logistical Selection Criteria (Lee & Lee, 2017)
From an operational perspective, Lee and Lee (2017) conducted an exploratory empirical study identifying the core facility attributes that event and exhibition organizers prioritize when selecting convention venues. Their findings established that logistical and physical capabilities—specifically **hall configuration flexibility, loading and setup efficiency, and rapid changeover capability**—rank among the highest determinants of venue viability, surpassing superficial decorative treatments. Their study validates that optimizing internal layout logistics and turnaround workflows directly enhances a facility's competitiveness and long-term usability.

## Cultural Identity

Convention centers are not just used for events; they can also become a platform for expressing cultural identity and fostering community pride. Several case studies highlight how architecture and programming can integrate and celebrate local culture.

In the Philippines, convention centers act as hubs of cultural and economic connectivity. Fulgar (2024) emphasizes that these venues are not just for commerce and trade but are pivotal spaces where Filipino culture and identity are showcased. The Philippine International Convention Center (PICC), designed by National Artist Leandro Locsin, remains an enduring symbol of the country’s global aspirations, cultural pride, and architectural heritage. As gathering spaces for diverse sectors, from policymakers to artists, Philippine convention centers promote knowledge exchange and community engagement, reinforcing local identity while supporting national development.

*(Figure 2-15. Photo of the Philippine International Convention Center | Source: PICC)*

At the Albuquerque Convention Center in New Mexico, cultural celebration is embedded deeply into its operation and design. The center regularly hosts multicultural events like Diwali, the Gathering of Nations, Mariachi Spectacular, and Juneteenth celebrations, positioning itself as a key venue for cultural exchange. It also integrates public art by New Mexican artists, with nearly 200 artworks displayed throughout the facility. These elements not only enrich visitor experience but also transform the center into a living museum of local and indigenous traditions, reinforcing a strong sense of place and belonging within the downtown Albuquerque community (ASM Global, 2024).

Heatherwick Studio’s redesign of Seoul’s Coex Convention Centre places a strong emphasis on cultural resonance and placemaking. Their winning proposal reimagines the venue as a “Cabinet of Curiosities,” moving away from its generic glass facade to a modular, inviting structure that promotes discovery and public interaction (Fakharany, 2025). By blending event spaces with public gardens, viewing decks, and cultural exhibits, the new Coex aims to serve not just event participants but the wider community. The design strategy intentionally strengthens ties with the surrounding park and neighborhood, ensuring that Coex becomes an integral, culturally relevant urban landmark even outside of major events.

### Provincial LGU Precedent: The Baguio Convention and Cultural Center (BCCC) Retrofit

While national flagships like the PICC and large-scale international venues illustrate macroeconomic scale and national identity, they operate under budgets and structural mandates far beyond those of provincial Local Government Units (LGUs). At the provincial municipal LGU scale, the rehabilitation and interior retrofitting of the **Baguio Convention and Cultural Center (BCCC)** (City Government of Baguio, 2019; DPWH, 2021; Filipina News Sentinel, 2022) provides a directly comparable, empirically grounded Philippine benchmark for civic interior modernization.

Facing operational challenges identical to the Calbayog facility, including acoustic reverberation, static spatial layouts, outdated lighting/rigging infrastructure, and generic interior surfaces, the Baguio City Government executed a comprehensive, non-structural interior retrofit within its aged 1977 municipal envelope. The project deployed modular acoustic wall paneling, flexible high-density seating systems, integrated cultural exhibition galleries, and restored Cordilleran architectural motifs—such as the iconic entrance *halipan* (rat guard) column geometries and indigenous art murals—without undertaking cost-prohibitive structural demolition. This precedent demonstrates that non-structural interior frameworks grounded in Open Building Theory (Habraken, 1972) and cultural placemaking are both operationally viable and fiscally appropriate for Philippine local government units seeking to future-proof their civic event infrastructure.

Taken together, these precedents show culturally responsive design operating at national (PICC), international placemaking (Coex), community and indigenous (Albuquerque), and provincial Philippine LGU (BCCC) scales. For the Calbayog City Convention Center, the equivalent move is to translate this principle to the Samar region, embedding local Basey *banig* weaving and waterfall heritage directly into non-structural interior surfaces, which is the focus of the following section.

## Culturally Responsive Design

Culturally responsive design is an approach that integrates the values, traditions, and spatial practices of a particular culture into the built environment, ensuring that architecture resonates with its users' identities and ways of life. This approach moves beyond aesthetics to address the deeper social and cultural meanings embedded in space, contributing to user well-being and social sustainability (Krstić, Filipović, & Ristić Trajković, 2024).

Kockler (1989), in her study on culturally responsive architecture for the Latino community in Roxbury, emphasized that design should reinforce core cultural attributes while adapting them to contemporary contexts. The failure to do so can lead to environments that feel alienating and disconnected from their users. Instead, she argued for spatial strategies that promote cultural continuity and empower communities in the design process.

The integration of these principles into interior design is particularly crucial, as interiors constitute the most immediate and intimate environments where individuals interact, live, and express identity. Kockler (1989) demonstrated that cultural values can be reflected in the use of specific materials, art forms like murals or mosaics, and spatial configurations that resonate with collective memory and heritage. Interior design thus becomes a medium through which occupants can recognize themselves in the built form.

While Kockler's study centers on domestic settings, its underlying principles transfer to civic and communal interiors, and it is those principles, rather than the specific residential forms, that this study draws on. Kockler (1989) illustrated the role of spatial organization in reinforcing cultural values through the prioritization of extended family structures in interior layouts, the use of open courtyards, and the delineation of space through a hierarchy from public to private zones. These configurations support cultural practices such as shared meals, intergenerational cohabitation, and communal interaction, which are practices that are similarly observed in many Filipino households and community structures.

The relevance of culturally responsive design extends beyond residential typologies to public buildings and community spaces. Interior layouts in such spaces must accommodate diverse functions (legal aid, education, recreation, and commerce) while remaining culturally resonant and emotionally engaging (Kockler, 1989). Similarly, Krstić et al. (2024) emphasized that emotionally responsive, culturally sensitive environments can enhance occupants’ sense of belonging and promote well-being. In modular or adaptive architectural typologies, this means creating flexible spatial arrangements that still reflect cultural narratives, symbols, and rhythms.

## Samar Weaving Heritage and Its Translation into Interior Surfaces

Within the Philippine context, the culturally-responsive principles above find a concrete anchor in the weaving heritage of Samar. Basey, Samar, is widely recognized as a center of *banig* (mat) weaving, a craft that uses locally grown *tikog* grass and functions simultaneously as a livelihood and as a marker of community identity passed down across generations (Garado et al., 2026; Del Monte et al., 2022). This living tradition supplies both a material vocabulary (woven fibers and mat textures) and a repertoire of geometric patterns that can inform interior surface treatments without dictating spatial layout.

Translating such heritage into the built environment is an established design method rather than a purely decorative gesture. Wagas (2016) demonstrates a replicable process for abstracting an indigenous weave into a reproducible pattern language, showing how a traditional motif can be systematically converted into a surface-design data set. At a broader scale, Baradas (1968) documents how a single regional motif, the Maranao *okir*, recurs coherently across weaving, carving, and architecture while carrying consistent symbolic meaning, an enduring precedent for embedding a local motif into architectural and interior surfaces. Del Monte (2021) situates this within contemporary Filipino practice, examining how designers negotiate imported design norms against the expression of local cultural identity in the built environment.

For the Calbayog City Convention Center, these sources support a strategy in which Samar's *banig* and *tikog* weaving motifs are abstracted and applied to upgraded perimeter acoustic drapery and ceiling acoustic overlays, so that the venue communicates a distinct sense of place while its layout remains fully reconfigurable. Documentation from the National Commission for Culture and the Arts on regional mat-making traditions further grounds this material selection (NCCA, 2004); however, a verifiable typology of specifically Waray/Eastern Visayas weaving motifs remains thin in the published literature and may warrant primary documentation (e.g., interviews with Basey weavers or the NCCA intangible-heritage inventory).

## Synthesis and Research Gap

### Theoretical & Empirical Synthesis

The reviewed literature establishes that contemporary convention and exhibition venues must evolve beyond rigid, single-purpose assembly halls into highly adaptable, culturally resonant environments (Ike et al., 2023; Ayuba & Agah, 2018; Lee & Lee, 2017). The physical viability of such spaces relies on the intersection of two foundational theories:
1. **Open Building Theory (Habraken, 1972)**, which delineates the permanent architectural envelope ("support") from reconfigurable interior systems ("kinetic infill")—such as modular FF&E, movable acoustic partitions, and overhead rigging grids.
2. **Theory of Place Attachment (Scannell & Gifford, 2010)**, operationalized through the Person-Process-Place (PPP) framework, which demonstrates that grounding public interiors in localized cultural narratives (e.g., Samar *banig* / *tikog* weaving and Calbayog waterfall iconography) cultivates civic pride, emotional connection, and sensory engagement without compromising spatial versatility (Del Monte, 2021; Kockler, 1989; Krstić et al., 2024).

### Explicit Statement of the Research Gap

Despite the extensive literature on the global MICE industry and modern convention architecture, a pronounced research gap persists: **the existing MICE and spatial design literature overwhelmingly emphasizes mega-scale, nationally funded international convention centers and capital-intensive structural expansions; non-structural interior adaptive strategies, layout standard manuals, and cultural abstraction frameworks for mid-scale, provincial municipal facilities remain critically under-researched.**

Most provincial Philippine LGUs operate aging or converted civic halls—such as the 840-square-meter Calbayog City Convention Center (comprising a 711 sqm Main Hall and 129 sqm Foyer), originally renovated from a municipal bus terminal in 2009—under strict capital constraints that preclude structural rebuilding. Existing literature fails to provide venue operators and interior designers with pre-calculated, non-structural spatial layout toolkits that simultaneously resolve rapid setup turnarounds, universal accessibility (BP 344), acoustic remediation, and regional cultural preservation within a fixed provincial envelope.

### Mapping of Research Sub-Problems (SPOP) to the Research Gap

To directly address this gap, this study formulates a site-specific interior design framework for the Calbayog City Convention Center, mapping each specific research sub-problem (SPOP) to the corresponding empirical and methodological deficit identified in the literature:

| Research Sub-Problem (SPOP) | Specific Literature / Empirical Gap Addressed | Methodological & Design Contribution |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **SPOP 1: Baseline IEQ & Spatial Operational Challenges**<br>*(Baseline IEQ satisfaction & operator bottlenecks in 840 sqm envelope: 711 sqm Main Hall & 129 sqm Foyer)* | Lack of empirical baseline studies documenting indoor environmental quality satisfaction (lighting, acoustics, thermal comfort), operational turnaround delays, and circulation friction in converted provincial municipal venues. | Conducts mixed-methods spatial triangulation (operator surveys, attendee IEQ evaluations, and spatial observation) to establish an empirical environmental and operational baseline for provincial civic halls. |
| **SPOP 2: Modular Zoning, FF&E & Universal Layout Typologies**<br>*(Plenary, Classroom, Banquet, Exhibition & BP 344 in 711 sqm hall + 129 sqm foyer)* | Absence of pre-calculated, anthropometrically calibrated layout templates and FF&E density ratios optimized for mid-scale (840 sqm) municipal event spaces under Philippine accessibility standards (BP 344/ADA). | Develops a standardized, repeatable interior layout manual integrating static/dynamic anthropometric clearances (Panero & Zelnik, 1979; Neufert, 2012) and BP 344 accessibility stalls. |
| **SPOP 3: Samar Heritage & Cultural Placemaking Translation**<br>*(Basey banig / tikog grass & waterfall linework)* | Generic "placelessness" in provincial public interiors; lack of formalized design protocols for abstracting Waray/Basey weaving and local natural iconography into non-structural, reconfigurable finishes. | Formulates a systematic Cultural Abstraction Matrix translating Basey *banig* patterns, *tikog* textures, and waterfall fluid dynamics into acoustic wall paneling and interior finishes (Scannell & Gifford, 2010; Del Monte, 2021). |
| **SPOP 4: Technical Future-Proofing, Acoustic Assemblies & Ceiling Tracks**<br>*(Acoustic overlays, kinetic tracks & material specs)* | Rapid functional obsolescence of municipal civic venues due to rigid specifications, passive ceiling planes, and lack of kinetic overhead infrastructure or sustainable acoustic assemblies. | Proposes a non-structural overhead ceiling track grid, sustainable local material specifications (*tikog* composites, FSC timber), and high-performance acoustic overlays to ensure multi-scale event versatility. |
