## Script

### Slide 1 · Title · 0:00–0:20

Good [morning/afternoon], panel members. Our study is Spatial Utilization Analysis and Evidence-Based Interior Design Interventions for the Calbayog Cultural Convention Center. In fifteen minutes: what the venue's data told us, what we designed from it, and how we tested it.

### Slide 2 · Rationale and SOP · 0:20–1:20

The Calbayog Cultural Convention Center is the city’s main air-conditioned civic hall: a former bus terminal, converted in 2009, with 840 square meters inside, a 711-square-meter Main Hall and a 129-square-meter lobby. The city chartered it for culture, arts and tourism, but it hosts everything: summits, trainings, galas and trade fairs. Each of those is built from scratch in one open hall with rented gear, and the room works against all of them. So we asked three questions. One: what is the baseline? How do attendees rate the indoor environment, and what slows crews down? Two: what layout types does the venue actually host, and which layouts serve them best? Three: how can the interior handle every event type while carrying Samar–Calbayog identity?

### Slide 3 · Significance and scope · 1:20–2:00

The study serves planners and crews with ready layouts and faster changeovers; the City Government and CACO with a venue that fits its cultural charter; attendees with a quieter, cooler, clearer hall; and designers with a retrofit model for provincial venues. The scope is the 840-square-meter interior only, and non-structural: no work on columns, facade or roof, no MEP engineering design, and no post-occupancy evaluation, because the design is not yet built.

### Slide 4 · Gap, implication, framework · 2:00–3:20

Convention-venue standards come from metropolitan flagships like PICC, Marina Bay and the Javits Center, where each function has its own hall. Provincial venues live in what our literature review calls a provincial reality gap: one shared hall, a small crew, lean budgets, and every event type in quick succession. What’s missing is a way to make that one hall both fast to convert and recognizably local. The implication is a non-structural retrofit model that other LGU venues can repeat without rebuilding. Our framework has two pillars. On the operations side, Actor-Network Theory and Affordance Theory: heavy tables, hard tiles and missing rigging points are non-human actors that resist the crew, so the design swaps them for ones that help. On the experience side, Place Attachment: banig, tikog, waterfall and Hadang motifs build attachment through the finishes themselves. Both lead to one outcome: a non-structural interior scheme for the 840 square meters.

### Slide 5 · Methodology · 3:20–4:40

We used applied design research with a convergent mixed-methods design, structured like an experiment. Phase one is the baseline: a 127-event ledger from 2014 to 2026, a survey of 124 attendees and 12 planners and operators, 10 key-informant interviews, shadowing of five live events from setup to teardown, and sound-decay tests at eight points following ISO 3382-2. Phase two is the intervention: we turned that evidence into layout templates and four non-structural modules, drawn to PD 1096, RA 9514, BP 344 and standard anthropometric clearances. Phase three is the test: every layout is checked against those codes and standards, and a nine-member stakeholder panel reviewed the designs in a focus group discussion. Throughout, Option A is the hall as it is used today and Option B is our proposal. The envelope, the non-structural boundary and the code limits stay fixed; those are our controls.

### Slide 6 · Utilization ledger · 4:40–5:25

Before designing, we asked what the hall is actually used for. Of 127 recorded events, nearly half are plenary assemblies, a quarter are classroom-style trainings, and the rest split between banquets and exhibitions. Size is bimodal: large convocations of 400 to 500 on one end, small workshops of 50 to 120 on the other. And 56.7 percent of all bookings fall in 2025 and 2026, so demand is growing. That’s why we designed four typologies at three scales, plus four hybrids: 13 scenarios.

> Source: Appendix A ledger; Tables 4.1b and 4.2.

### Slide 7 · Baseline: IEQ and turnaround · 5:25–6:10

Attendees rated the current hall below the midpoint on every comfort item. Thermal comfort is lowest at 2.17, then layout and legroom at 2.51 and sightlines at 2.54; the composite is 2.54 out of 5, from 124 respondents. Planners see the same hall from the other side: 10 of 12 need four hours or more to set up, three of them more than twelve. Their top friction, at 4.42, is the lack of overhead rigging points: every truss, light and speaker has to stand on the floor.

> Values recomputed from the Google Forms exports (attendees N = 124, planners n = 12). See the errata backup slide.

### Slide 8 · Acoustics · 6:10–6:55

We also measured the room. With interrupted pink noise, the empty Main Hall averages a 2.53-second decay, three seconds at the stage front, against a speech target of 0.8 to 1.1 seconds. That’s 130 percent over. The causes: 711 square meters of glazed tile, an unbaffled ceiling and bare gypsum bulkheads. Live events show the trade-off: at the Samar Investment Forum, hanging fabric to tame the echo pushed the sound crew to 110.5 dBA at the front row.

> If asked: decay was timed by stopwatch in 0.5 s steps, three trials per point, so it is an approximation of ISO 3382-2. That is why the proposed value (≈ 0.95 s) is presented as modeled, not measured.

### Slide 9 · Observed bottlenecks · 6:55–7:45

We shadowed five live events and audited eight more through archival photos; each is mapped as a zoning diagram, Figures 4.1 to 4.13, in the annex. Six bottlenecks repeat. Only one entrance leaf opens, so a two-meter door works as a one-meter door. There’s no loading dock, so deliveries cross the guest entry. Restrooms sit behind the stage with no signs. Ground trusses take about 36 square meters, or 40 seats. Taped cables cross the aisles. And drapes without tiebacks hide the side exits, which have no exit signs.

> Point to each numbered marker on the plan as you name it.

### Slide 10 · Cultural findings · 7:45–8:15

Attendees also told us what the room should say. Every cultural item scored above 4.2, and expressing authentic Samar culture scored 4.52. Interviews gave three sources, banig and tikog weaving, Calbayog’s waterfalls and the Hadang festival, plus one rule from planners: keep finishes neutral so any event can style over them.

### Slide 12 · Design rules and modules · 11:20–12:15

Everything in the video follows one rule set, Table 4.14. Theater rows at 900-millimeter pitch, staggered so each person sees between two heads. Aisles of 1,500 to 1,800 millimeters. Banquet service lanes of 1,620 to 1,800, so a waiter with a full tray never brushes a chair. Four modules carry it. Module 1, the layout templates. Module 2, the cultural acoustic finishes, modeled to bring decay from 2.53 to about 0.95 seconds. Module 3, the overhead utility grid, which removes floor trusses and gives back 40 seats. Module 4, the air-conditioned lobby concourse with eight two-by-two-meter stalls.

### Slide 13 · 13-scenario test suite · 12:15–13:00

The rules produce 13 test scenarios: each typology at peak, mid and compact scale, plus four hybrids drawn from real co-programmed events like the cookfest and the investment forum. Each scenario has an Option A, the setup we observed, and an Option B, our layout. All 13 pairs are in the annex. We’ll walk through two: the biggest single-use case, and the most complex hybrid.

> Point to 3A, then 4D.

### Slide 14 · Scenario 3A · 13:00–13:45

Scenario 3A, the full civic gala. Option A is Hermana Night: 450 to 500 guests on a grid, truss towers on the floor, waiters squeezing between chairs. The building code’s 1.4 square meters per diner would allow about 507, but once you hold 1,620 to 1,800-millimeter service lanes, the real ceiling is 300. Option B gives two templates: 30 round tables of ten at 3.6-meter centers, or long communal tables along a processional aisle for fiesta galas. Overhead battens replace the trusses and return 40 seats.

### Slide 15 · Scenario 4D · 13:45–14:30

Scenario 4D, banquet plus expo. Option A is the Samar Investment Forum: a lobby above 28 degrees, a display table blocking the second door leaf, and overflow stalls out on the driveway. Option B cools the lobby to 22 to 24 degrees with two split units, sets eight two-by-two-meter stalls on fabric tracks, keeps two meters clear at both doors, and runs the banquet in the hall with 1,800-millimeter lanes. Every exhibit comes indoors, and the exits stay clear.

### Slide 16 · Validation · 14:30–15:40

How do we know Option B works before it’s built? First, every layout is checked against the standards it was drawn from: BP 344 routes, turning space and wheelchair bays with companion seats, with a movable platform lift instead of a long ramp; RA 9514 exit clearances; the sightline rule; the banquet spacing ceiling; and a Sabine model for reverberation. Second, the nine-member stakeholder panel reviewed all 13 scenarios. Their review led to six refinements, listed in Table 4.16: layout sheets keyed to the column grid, a rear catering lane, overhead power drops, a solid acoustic wall at Grid B, cultural plaques, and a demountable stage with a zero-stage mode that frees 52.5 square meters.

### Slide 17 · Conclusion · 15:40–17:00

Three conclusions. One: the hall’s bottlenecks are not crew errors; they’re built into its objects, the tiles, heavy tables and missing rigging, so changing the objects changes the operations. Two: an aging civic hall doesn’t need structural work to perform. Non-structural modules can bring changeovers of venue furniture and rigging under 90 minutes, a projection since it isn’t built, while clearing exits and recovering seats. Three: Samar identity and flexibility aren’t in conflict; abstracted crafts in neutral, fire-rated acoustic finishes do both jobs. We recommend that the City Government adopt the layout manual and buy wheeled furniture and demountable staging; that City Engineering certify ceiling loads, reopen the second door leaves and add dedicated circuits; and that CACO run rotating artisan exhibits. Future research should run a post-occupancy evaluation at 6, 12 and 24 months after fit-out, and test the acoustic model with simulation software.

### Slide 18 · Final pitch · 17:00–19:00

Picture the next city-wide convocation at the Calbayog Cultural Convention Center: four hundred to five hundred people. Today the crew starts the day before. Trucks unload through the guest entrance, trusses go up on the floor, cables are taped across the aisles, and when it’s over the whole crowd leaves through one door leaf. In between, guests sit in a hall that echoes for two and a half seconds and runs hot; they rated it 2.54 out of 5.

Our proposal changes that without touching a single column. Layout templates show the crew exactly where everything goes. A ceiling grid lifts lights and speakers off the floor and gives back 40 seats. Banig, tikog and waterfall-inspired acoustic finishes are modeled to bring the echo down to about one second, and they make the room unmistakably Samar. A cooled lobby brings every exhibit indoors.

For the City Government, that is a venue that finally matches its charter as a cultural convention center. For planners and crews, changeovers projected under 90 minutes. For the people of Calbayog, a hall they are proud to show visitors: civic pride was the highest-rated item in our survey, at 4.65.

And it can start now. Adopt the layout manual and reopen the second door leaves; both cost little. Then add wheeled furniture and modular staging. Then the ceiling grid and acoustic finishes, once City Engineering certifies the loads.

This hall already hosts Calbayog’s biggest moments. Let’s give it an interior worthy of them. Thank you.

## Q&A preparation

**"Why do your slide figures differ from the submitted tables?"**
We recomputed every survey figure directly from the raw Google Forms exports, and the submitted tables did not match them. The errata sheet lists each change. *(Slide 19.)*

**"How did you measure turnaround for a design that isn't built?"**
We didn't measure it; it is a projection. The baseline is measured: 10 of 12 planners need four hours or more. The modules remove the steps that take that time: erecting ground trusses, taping floor cables, and measuring layouts by hand. A post-occupancy evaluation would measure the real figure, which is why it's our first recommendation.

**"The code allows about 507 diners. Why cap the banquet at 300?"**
711 m² ÷ 1.40 m² per diner ≈ 507 under PD 1096. That figure ignores service. After the stage, buffets and perimeter aisles, about 420–450 m² is left for dining, and keeping 1,620–1,800 mm lanes between ten-seat rounds fits 30 tables, or 300 guests. Hermana Night tried close to 500, and waiters could not get through.

**"How accurate is your reverberation measurement?"**
It's an interrupted-noise test after ISO 3382-2: a pink-noise source at center stage, a Benetech GM1352 meter, three trials at each of eight points, decay timed by stopwatch to 0.5 s. That is enough to show the hall is more than double the target, but not precise, so the proposed 0.95 s is presented as a Sabine model, not a measurement. *(Slide 20.)*

**"Is an unbuilt design really an experiment?"**
Groat and Wang (2013) list simulation research as an experimental strategy for buildings. The baseline is the pre-test, the design is the treatment, and the code checks plus the stakeholder review are the post-test (Tremblay, Hevner & Berndt, 2010, on focus groups for evaluating unbuilt designs). The envelope, the non-structural boundary and the code limits are held constant.

**"Why a focus group and not a post-occupancy evaluation?"**
A POE needs a built space. Pre-construction validity comes from the code checks and the stakeholder review, and the POE is recommended for 6, 12 and 24 months after fit-out.

**"What is your instrument's reliability?"**
Cronbach's α of 0.9469 (attendee form) and 0.9402 (planner form) come from the **pilot test**; say "pilot". On the main attendee sample, per-cluster α ranges from 0.45 to 0.67. The planner sample (n = 12) is too small for a stable α. Don't claim 0.94 for the main sample.

**"Where are Figures 4.1–4.30?"**
They are cited in Tables 4.5d-2 and 4.15 of the submitted copy, printed in the annex in front of you, and will be added to the final manuscript.
